title: "The Month Utah Ran on Sunlight"

In May 2026, for the first time, photovoltaic panels in Utah produced more electricity than any other source. Solar accounted for nearly one terawatt-hour—about a third of the state’s generation—edging out natural gas at 32% and coal at 28%. The event is easy to dismiss as a statistical quirk: good weather, a few big farms coming online, a month when spring sun happened to align with low wind. But it is also a quiet inflection point, the kind you only recognize in retrospect. Utah, a state famous for coal, Mormon pioneers, and an uneasy relationship with environmental regulation, just had a month in which the sun powered more of its economy than anything dug from the ground.

That fact matters because energy transitions are usually invisible. We think of them as policy fights—tariffs, tax credits, permits, executive orders—and they are. But underneath the politics, the hardware keeps getting installed. The Green River Energy Center, a 2,500-acre solar and storage complex in Emery County, came online this spring with 400 megawatts of panels and 400 megawatts of batteries. Excelsior Energy Capital’s Faraday Solar project in Utah County is now feeding up to 685 megawatts to Meta’s local data center. Elektron Solar, an 80-megawatt farm in Tooele County, has been supplying Salt Lake City, Park City, and Summit County since mid-2024. These are not boutique installations. They are grid-scale infrastructure, bolted into the same transmission network that once carried only coal and gas.

Dan Schroeder, a physics professor at Weber State University who tracks the data, called the milestone “a curiosity.” He is right, in the narrow sense that one month does not make a trend. But the broader trend is unmistakable. Schroeder himself pointed it out: the steady growth of solar in Utah is “wonderful news for air quality, it’s wonderful news for the climate, and it’s wonderful news for jobs and the economy.” Utah now has nearly 8,000 solar jobs across 132 companies, and investors committed $1.5 billion to the state’s solar market in 2025 alone. Those are real payrolls and real capital flows, not aspirational forecasts on a campaign website.

What strikes me is how normal this is starting to feel. A decade ago, a state like Utah getting a third of its power from the sun would have been front-page proof of a green-energy revolution. In 2026 it is a respectable local story, buried under the larger noise of federal policy reversals, tariff fights, and a presidential administration that calls wind and solar “expensive and unreliable.” The White House is phasing out tax incentives and has told federal agencies to unwind support for renewable projects. PacifiCorp, parent of Rocky Mountain Power, has responded by pulling back its own planned wind and solar builds over the next two decades, citing changed federal priorities.

Yet the plants keep producing. Meta still wants clean power for its data center. Solar and battery combinations are still undercutting fossil generation on cost in many markets. And the sun still rises over Emery County every morning. That is the thing about infrastructure: once it is built, it creates its own constituency. Workers know how to maintain it, financiers know how to refinance it, and grid operators learn how to dispatch it. Unbuilding it is harder than building it, and usually more expensive.

There is a useful analogy here with California, which in the same May period got roughly 51% of its electricity from solar. California’s lead is not because Utah is failing; it is because California started earlier, built more transmission, and pushed harder through regulation and incentives. Utah is now on the same curve, just later. The relevant question is not whether Utah will catch California but whether it will keep climbing the curve fast enough to matter for climate and air quality. Schroeder thinks it can. “Utah still has room to grow its solar percentage,” he said. Given the state’s sunny, dry climate, that is a statement of physics as much as economics.

Logan Mitchell, a climate scientist with Utah Clean Energy, put the May milestone in a wider seasonal context. Wind generation, he noted, had already overtaken every other source in PacifiCorp’s Utah-Wyoming-Idaho portfolio during December and January, thanks to new turbines including 400 megawatts at the Rock Creek II facility. Wind and solar complement each other: wind peaks in winter, solar in summer. Together they are starting to look less like intermittent novelties and more like a paired baseload system, buffered by the 400 megawatts of battery storage at Green River alone.

Mitchell’s phrase for this is “liftoff.” That is a strong word, but it fits. Liftoff is the moment when a technology stops being pushed by subsidy and starts being pulled by economics. It is also the moment when opponents stop arguing that the thing is impossible and start arguing that it is happening too fast, or in the wrong places, or for the wrong reasons. We are already there. The current debate is less about whether renewables work and more about who profits, who pays, and how quickly old assets should be retired.

That debate is legitimate. Energy transitions create losers. Coal communities in Utah have spent generations building an identity around mining and power-plant jobs. Gas producers, pipeline operators, and utilities with long-lived fossil assets face real financial risk. A renewable-dominated grid also raises hard engineering questions about transmission, storage, seasonal balancing, and resilience during extreme weather. None of those problems solve themselves.

But pretending the transition is not happening is worse than managing it poorly. The federal policy of the moment seems designed to slow the build-out by removing incentives and casting renewables as foreign-controlled and unreliable. The irony is that many of the projects now producing Utah’s solar power were financed, approved, and built during the same policy era the administration is trying to undo. The tax credits may shrink, but the sunk cost remains. A solar farm does not stop working because a president dislikes it.

This is where my own bias shows. I think the United States is better off accelerating the transition than fighting it, not for ideological reasons but for pragmatic ones. Domestic solar and wind reduce exposure to global fuel-price shocks. They reduce the air pollution that sends people to emergency rooms. They keep dollars inside local economies instead of shipping them to methane exporters. And they buy time against the climate impacts that are already arriving: hot springs, cold snaps, shrinking snowpack, longer tick seasons. Mitchell keeps a running list of those impacts. It is not a future-tense story anymore; it is a present-tense one.

The honest counterargument is that reliability still matters. The sun does not shine at night; the wind does not always blow. Batteries help, but they are not yet cheap enough at multi-day scale to fully cover seasonal gaps. Gas plants will likely remain as backup for years. Coal may hang on longer than climate advocates want. A grid that is 51% solar in May can still struggle in a calm December evening. Those are engineering and investment problems, not reasons to stop. The way through them is more transmission, more storage, more demand flexibility, and—yes—some remaining fossil backup while the cleaner system matures.

What Utah’s May numbers suggest is that the clean-energy share of the mix is becoming structurally significant. When a single source crosses a third of generation in a month, it is no longer a garnish. Grid operators have to plan around it, utilities have to price around it, and politicians have to account for the voters who depend on it. That is what liftoff looks like in practice: not a parade, just a quiet reordering of whose interests count.

I keep returning to the word “curiosity,” because Schroeder chose it carefully. A one-month milestone is not destiny. But it is evidence. And evidence has a way of piling up until the story changes. Utah may still vote for politicians who disparage renewable energy, and its utilities may still file plans that assume a slower transition. Yet every morning, more electrons flowing through its wires come from panels installed on former grazing land and empty desert valleys than from the coal seams that built the state’s industrial identity. That is not a curiosity. That is a signal.

The question now is whether Utah—and the country—reads the signal correctly, or spends the next decade pretending the sun did not just win a month.
