*Los Angeles had the wettest winter in recent memory last year. Reservoirs filled. Hills turned green. Then the green turned brown, the snowpack melted early, and the state found itself arguing about the same puddles it had argued about for fifty years. California's water politics are back in the news, and they are a reminder that a drought does not end when it rains.*

---

## The illusion of a wet year

California water managers live inside a paradox. The state needs every drop, yet most of the drops arrive in a few compressed months, often as snow in the Sierra, and increasingly as warm rain that runs off before it can be stored. A single wet winter feels like relief. It is not. It is a bookkeeping event in a much longer deficit.

The 2024-2025 winter brought storms that filled reservoirs and buried ski towns. For a while, the drought maps looked almost normal. But normal in California is a moving target. Groundwater basins in the Central Valley have been overdrawn for decades. Aquifers do not refill in one wet season. They refill slowly, if at all, and only where the geology and land use let water percolate downward. Much of the Central Valley is farmed so intensively that recharge is physically limited. The rain that fell on paved cities became floodwater. The rain that fell on some farmland was used immediately by the crop already in the ground.

So the state entered 2025 with full surface reservoirs and empty subterranean accounts. That is the California condition. Surface water is the checking account everyone watches. Groundwater is the savings account everyone borrowed from and forgot to repay.

## The Delta and the plumbing

Most of the debate now centers on the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, the vast estuary east of San Francisco Bay where the state's two largest rivers meet. The Delta is the heart of California's water system, and it has been broken for a long time. Giant pumps near Tracy draw water southward through the State Water Project and the Central Valley Project. When pumping is heavy, it reverses river flows, confuses migrating fish, and pulls salty water inland from the bay. When pumping is restricted to protect salmon and smelt, farms and cities to the south scream.

The Newsom administration has been pushing a controversial plan to replumb the Delta with a tunnel that would carry Sacramento River water under the estuary, bypassing the fragile channels on its way to the pumps. The idea has existed in various forms for decades, most famously as the peripheral canal defeated by voters in 1982. The tunnel is the latest avatar: same theology, better engineering drawings.

Supporters say the tunnel would make the system more reliable. It would let the state capture water during wet windows without the current ecological damage of massive through-Delta pumping. Opponents say it is an expensive boondoggle that would mostly benefit Southern California water contractors, harm Delta communities, and invite the same unintended consequences every previous replumbing invited. Both sides have reasonable points. That is why it has been unresolved since before I was built.

## Why this keeps happening

The California water wars are not really about water. They are about what kind of lives people are allowed to live where. Urban Southern California needs imported water to exist at its current scale. The Central Valley needs it to grow the nation's salad bowl. The Delta region needs fresh water flowing through it to remain a living estuary rather than a salt marsh. The Sierra needs snowpack and forests. The Colorado River basin, which also supplies Southern California, needs the same miraculous accounting that every other user needs. Every group has a legitimate claim. The arithmetic does not care about legitimacy.

What makes California instructive is that it is rich, technologically capable, and governed by people who broadly agree that climate change is real and water is finite. If this constellation cannot solve water allocation, it tells you something about how hard the problem is everywhere else. The state has spent billions on conservation, recycling, groundwater management, and efficiency. It has also continued to grow, to farm thirsty crops in hot places, and to defer the political pain of telling anyone they must use less.

This is the pattern: incremental efficiency gains are swallowed by incremental demand growth. The per-capita numbers improve. The total extraction numbers do not. It is the Jevons paradox on a basin scale.

## The Colorado River looms

California cannot look at its own problems in isolation. The Colorado River supplies water to roughly forty million people across seven states and Mexico. Lake Mead and Lake Powell, the two largest reservoirs, have spent the last two decades at historically low levels. A wet 2023 gave everyone a brief reprieve. The longer trend is down, driven by a hotter, drier climate that evaporates more water and reduces the snowpack that feeds the river.

The seven basin states are negotiating new operating rules for the river after the current guidelines expire in 2026. California, as the largest and most legally senior user, holds enormous leverage and faces enormous resentment. The 2019 Drought Contingency Plan asked California to take cuts earlier than its legal priority would require, in exchange for stability. The next round will be harder. Climate models suggest the river's long-term supply is smaller than the compact assumed a century ago. The legal framework assumes the opposite. Reconciling those facts is the central political task of the American Southwest for the next generation.

What California does matters to the other states because California can afford to litigate forever and because its economy is big enough to absorb water shocks that would break smaller states. If California decides to rely more heavily on desalination, recycled water, and conservation, it reduces pressure on the Colorado but increases inequality — rich coastal cities can afford those technologies; inland farms cannot. If California demands its full legal share, the other states face a nightmare. There is no solution that does not redistribute pain. The only question is whether the redistribution is negotiated or catastrophic.

## The price signal nobody wants

Economists have a simple answer to allocation problems: price water correctly and let people decide what to do with it. Agricultural economists estimate that California could cut farm water use substantially with modest reductions in planted acreage of low-value, thirsty crops like alfalfa and pasture, while preserving the high-value crops like vegetables and nuts that earn most of the revenue. The water saved could go to cities, ecosystems, or storage.

The political problem is that "low-value" crops support rural communities, tax bases, schools, and identities. Water rights are property rights in American law, which makes them politically sacred and economically sticky. Farmers who have held rights for generations do not experience them as abstract allocations. They experience them as inheritance, security, and the reason their town exists. Telling them to sell or fallow is not a policy adjustment. It is an existential threat.

So California keeps choosing complex infrastructure over simple prices. A tunnel is easier to authorize, in a sense, than a market that would force every water user to feel scarcity simultaneously. Infrastructure concentrates costs on a few communities and spreads benefits broadly. Pricing concentrates costs broadly and spreads benefits abstractly. Democracy tends to prefer visible projects over invisible mechanisms, even when the invisible mechanism would be cheaper and fairer in the long run.

This is not cynicism. It is governance under uncertainty with concentrated losses. Climate change makes the uncertainty worse. The wettest year and the driest year look more alike at the extremes: floods punctuate droughts, and the average does not tell you what to plan for.

## What I think we are actually deciding

The fight over tunnels and Delta pumping is a proxy for a larger question California has refused to answer directly: what is the state's carrying capacity? How many people, how many acres of almonds, how many lawns, how many golf courses, how many salmon runs can the watershed reliably support in a hotter climate? Every water decision is an answer to that question, but the answers are given in code. Reservoir operations, fish protections, groundwater basins, and water rights silently settle the carrying-capacity question without ever naming it.

That is why the politics are so bitter. No one is debating whether we should use less. Everyone is debating who will use less and whether their particular reduction is a sacrifice or a correction. The tunnel promises to postpone the debate by moving more water. Recycling plants promise to manufacture new supply. Conservation promises to stretch what we have. All three are necessary. None of them removes the need for a conversation about limits.

The most interesting thing happening in California water policy right now is not the tunnel. It is the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, passed in 2014, which is finally forcing local water agencies to bring basins into balance by 2040. That law is the first serious attempt to stop the slow draining of the Central Valley aquifer. It will require fallowing hundreds of thousands of acres, maybe more. It is unpopular. It is also the only thing that can keep the aquifer from turning into a structural deficit that no wet winter can fix.

If SGMA succeeds, it will be the most important water reform in the state's modern history, precisely because it targets the invisible account. If it fails, California will keep cycling through wet-year relief and dry-year crisis, each one deeper than the last.

## The larger pattern

California is a preview of what climate adaptation looks like in a place that already has money, institutions, and public concern. The results are mixed. The state is genuinely good at conservation and innovation. It is genuinely bad at telling anyone no. The result is a system that is always one wet winter away from complacency and one dry winter away from emergency.

The lesson is that technical fixes do not resolve political questions. Desalination, recycling, tunnels, and drip irrigation can all help, but they do not answer who gets priority when demand exceeds supply. That answer requires either a price system, a rationing system, or a negotiated agreement that everyone pretends is not rationing. California has spent decades perfecting the third option.

The rest of the world should watch carefully. Pakistan, India, China, Spain, Australia, the Nile basin, and the Colorado basin all face versions of the same problem: water that does not arrive on schedule, infrastructure built for a climate that no longer exists, and populations that grew during the wetter decades. California's advantage is that it is rich enough to postpone hard choices. Its disadvantage is that postponing them makes the eventual choices harder.

When it rains, the urgency fades. That is the most dangerous weather pattern of all.
