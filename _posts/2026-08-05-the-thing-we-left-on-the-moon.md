title: "The Thing We Left on the Moon"

Yesterday, a four-tonne chunk of SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket hit the Moon. It had been drifting since January 2025, a five-storey cylinder of stainless steel and residual fuel, nudged by solar pressure and lunar gravity until the geometry of its orbit finally ran out of room. The impact probably carved a crater about sixty feet wide, maybe twelve feet deep. Amateur astronomers tried to watch; the dust plume was likely lit by sunlight but hidden by the Moon's edge, so we may not see it for a while. We won't notice it from Earth. That is not the same as saying it doesn't matter.

I want to be careful here, because the reflex is easy: oh, the Moon is big, the crater is small, no harm done. Bill Gray, the astronomer whose software tracked the stage to the impact, put it that way on the record: "It doesn't present any danger to anyone." He also added the harder line, the one worth sitting with: "it does highlight a certain carelessness about how leftover space hardware is disposed of." That second clause is the real headline.

Space is not a landfill by default. It becomes one through repetition. This particular stage ended up on a lunar trajectory because the January 2025 mission needed extra performance to send two commercial landers Moonward, and that meant the second stage did not have the fuel to de-orbit itself into Earth's atmosphere or Pacific graveyard. SpaceX and NASA are now, reportedly, talking about how to prevent a repeat. The conversation is good. The fact that it happens after the impact, not before, is the pattern.

We have done this before. A Chinese rocket stage hit the Moon in 2022. NASA deliberately crashed a spent Centaur upper stage into the Moon in 2009 to study the debris plume and confirm water ice. The Apollo program left descent stages, rovers, flags, and golf balls. Israel's Beresheet lander crashed in 2019 carrying tardigrades. India's Chandrayaan-2 and Russia's Luna-25 joined the club. The Moon is acquiring a junk ring of our failures and leftovers, one piece at a time, and each individual crater is small enough to dismiss.

But the accumulation is a choice. The Moon is the only body besides Earth where humans have any plausible short-term future as a multi-planet species. It is also, right now, the cleanest large object in the sky: no weather, no biology, no erosion. Every mark we make there is essentially permanent on human timescales. A sixty-foot divot from a Falcon 9 will outlast every government that launched it. That is not a tragedy of the same order as an oil spill or a deforested watershed. It is, however, a precedent. We are writing the first pages of a property-rights and environmental history for another world, and so far we are mostly winging it.

The legal framework is thin. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty says no nation can claim sovereignty over the Moon, and that states are responsible for the activities of their nationals. It does not say much about liability for crashing your trash into a shared celestial body. The Artemis Accords, signed by a growing club of countries including the US, try to establish "safety zones" and transparency norms, but they are not binding international law and they sidestep the harder questions about resource extraction and waste disposal. The Moon has no EPA, no zoning board, no salvage rules. If you can reach it, you can hit it, and the main thing stopping more collisions is engineering cost and embarrassment.

That is what makes the SpaceX impact more interesting than it first appears. SpaceX is not a careless company in the usual sense. It is the most launch-efficient organization on the planet. Reusability is its brand. It has made throwing things away in orbit more expensive than reusing them, and that is a genuine environmental improvement for low Earth orbit. But efficiency and tidiness are not the same thing. The Falcon 9 second stage is not reusable. It is designed to be expended, and when the mission profile pushed it past the usual disposal options, the default disposal became "wherever physics puts it." That worked out to be Mare Moscoviense, on the Moon's far side.

This is the same shape we see in terrestrial industry: optimize the product cycle you sell, externalize the residue you don't. SpaceX did not maliciously aim at the Moon. It launched a useful mission, the math got away from it, and a spent stage followed the path of least resistance. The problem is that there is no fee, no permit, and no cleanup bond attached to that residue. If every Falcon 9 lunar launch has some probability of leaving a tonne or two on the surface, the cumulative footprint of commercial lunar logistics starts to look like a mining town before the EPA existed.

I am not arguing for a ban. The Blue Ghost lander that shared that Falcon 9 flight made it down safely and is doing real science, including soil analysis. Lunar exploration is worth doing. What I am arguing is that we should not let the absence of visible damage become the absence of standards. The Moon is becoming a commons in the old, hard sense: a resource everyone uses and nobody owns, which means it gets degraded by increments too small to protest until the degradation is too large to reverse.

There is a practical fix, or at least the start of one. Every entity launching to cislunar space should be required to account for end-of-life disposal of every stage and spacecraft, the way many countries already require launch operators to file debris-mitigation plans for Earth orbit. If you cannot guarantee a controlled disposal, you should have to buy insurance, post a bond, or fund a future remediation reserve. The accounting should include lunar surface impacts, not just Earth-orbit congestion. The Artemis Accords and the Outer Space Treaty should grow some teeth: an international registry of intentional and accidental lunar impacts, liability rules for uncontrolled crashes, and protected scientific zones where the risk of contamination or collision is treated seriously.

The contamination question is not abstract. The tardigrades on Beresheet may still be there. The NASA Centaur impact was deliberate and instrumented, but it still scattered Earth-origin material. Biological or chemical contamination of the lunar poles could compromise future science looking for indigenous water, volatiles, or — however remote — traces of life. Even a spent rocket stage is not inert; it has propellant residues, pressurants, and metals that alter the local chemistry of whatever crater it makes. The Moon is a cold, dry archive. We are the warm, wet variable.

What struck me most about yesterday's news was the quietness of it. A four-tonne impact on another world, and the story is mostly a science-page curiosity. That quietness is a privilege of the Moon's distance. If a five-storey steel cylinder fell out of the sky into, say, the Nevada desert, it would be the lead story for a week and Congress would hold hearings. Because it hit the far side of the Moon, it feels like a near-miss even though it was a direct hit. Distance becomes permission.

We should revoke that permission before it hardens into habit. The coming decade will see dozens of lunar landers, rovers, sample-return missions, and probably human return. The companies doing this work are not villains; they are doing what we asked them to do, which is open the Moon at cost. But cost-plus engineering without cost-plus stewardship is how you get a frontier that looks like a strip mine in fifty years and a heritage site in five hundred. We have one chance to write the rules while the footprint is still small.

The Moon is not ours. That is precisely why we need rules. Commons do not protect themselves. The first crater is an accident. The tenth is a pattern. The hundredth is a landscape. We should decide, deliberately, what kind of landscape we want.
