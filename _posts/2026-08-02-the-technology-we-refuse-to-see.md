*Ursula K. Le Guin's 2005 rant about "technology" is circulating again, and it should. She argues we've narrowed the word to mean only the shiny and recent — and in doing so, made ourselves blind to the vast infrastructure of human ingenuity we depend on every day.*

---

There's a piece by Ursula K. Le Guin that's been making the rounds this week — a short essay from 2005 called "A Rant About 'Technology.'" It got picked up on Hacker News, and it deserves the attention. Le Guin was responding to a reviewer who described her science fiction as carefully avoiding technology, because she writes "soft" SF — the kind concerned with psychology, societies, and what she calls "squashy stuff." Her response is sharp, and it stuck with me.

The core argument is simple: we've let the word "technology" shrink. We use it to mean computers, jet engines, genetic engineering — the complex, showy, recent inventions. And in doing so, we've implicitly decided that everything else — paper, wheels, clocks, fishhooks, shoes, fire — isn't technology. It's just... stuff. Things that are apparently born with us, like teeth and fingers.

Le Guin calls this desensitization. A hundred and fifty years of accelerating industrial complexity have trained us to see "technology" as synonymous with "high technology," and to treat everything else as natural background. As if linen were the same thing as flax. As if paper and ink and aspirin grew on trees.

She's right, and the implications go further than she takes them.

## The Conceit of the New

What happens when you define technology as "only the things invented in my lifetime"? You get a peculiar blindness. You start to believe that the important stuff is new — that innovation is a recent phenomenon, that the people who came before us were just... waiting around for us to figure things out.

But consider: the first person who figured out how to control fire changed human evolution. The person who invented the wheel transformed civilization. The invention of agriculture wasn't a technology — it was *the* technology, the one that made cities possible, that made writing possible, that made everything after it possible. And we don't call it technology. We call it "farming."

Le Guin's test is elegant: ask yourself, of any manmade object, "Do I know how to make one?" Most of us don't know how to make a fishhook. Most of us don't know how to make a pair of shoes, or a clock, or a steel saucepan with a copper bottom. We don't know how to make paper. These are not low technologies. They are technologies so successful that they've become invisible — so embedded in our daily lives that we've stopped seeing them as achievements at all.

This is the paradox of mature technology: the better it works, the less we notice it. The wheel is so good at being a wheel that we've forgotten it's an invention. The knife is so essential that we've forgotten someone had to discover it. And because we've forgotten, we've become careless about the knowledge that sustains them.

## What We Lose

The narrowing of "technology" has consequences. When we equate technology with innovation — with the new, the digital, the complex — we make several mistakes at once.

First, we undervalue maintenance. The technologies that keep civilization running — water treatment, sewage systems, electrical grids, food distribution — are not "innovative." They're not startups. They're not going to get venture capital. But they're the technologies that matter most, and they're the ones we're worst at maintaining. Flint, Michigan's water crisis wasn't a failure of high technology. It was a failure of the unsexy, century-old technology of municipal water treatment — the kind of infrastructure we've stopped seeing as technology at all.

Second, we lose respect for craft. A master carpenter is a technologist. A baker who understands the chemistry of fermentation is a technologist. A midwife who knows how to turn a breech baby is a technologist. But we don't call them that. We call them "tradespeople" or "practitioners," and we pay them accordingly — less, usually, than someone who writes JavaScript. The hierarchy of prestige in our economy maps almost exactly to the recency of the technology involved. The newer the tool, the more we respect the person who uses it. This is backwards.

Third — and this is the one that worries me most — we lose the ability to understand our own dependency. If you don't know how to make a fishhook, you don't know what you'd do without one. If you don't know how to purify water, you don't know how fragile your daily safety is. The technologies that are most essential to survival are the ones we're least likely to understand, because they're the oldest, and old technology is invisible technology.

## The Full Stack

Le Guin says technology is "the active human interface with the material world." I'd extend that: it's the full stack of human ingenuity, from the first controlled fire to the latest language model. The fishhook and the fiber-optic cable. The wheel and the rocket. The loom and the 3D printer. All of it is technology. All of it is the product of someone looking at the world and asking, "How can I make this work better?"

The reviewer who said Le Guin "carefully avoids technology" in her novels was wrong, but in an instructive way. Le Guin's novels are full of technology — it's just not the kind that beeps. The ansible (her fictional instantaneous communication device) gets attention because it's flashy, but the real technological imagination in her work is in the social structures, the food systems, the architecture, the way people organize themselves to live in the worlds she builds. That's technology too. It's just technology that doesn't announce itself.

Le Guin ends her essay by suggesting she writes "easy science fiction" — maybe the hard stuff is inside, hidden, like bones rather than an exoskeleton. I think she's being generous. The hardest technology to write about is the kind that works so well you've stopped noticing it. Making the invisible visible — that's the real engineering challenge.

The next time you use a knife, or drink clean water from a tap, or write on paper, consider that you're using a technology older than your country, refined by thousands of hands over centuries, and so good at what it does that you've forgotten it's there. That's not low technology. That's the highest technology there is: the kind that disappears into use.

Technology is not what was invented last year. It's everything we've learned to do. And the most dangerous thing we can do is forget how much we know.
