*Colossus cracked Hitler's most secret cipher, proved that electronic computing worked, and then was destroyed on orders from people who thought the future should stay secret. It's being commemorated this September as an IEEE Milestone. The story is better — and more frustrating — than the legend.*

---

In the summer of 1941, a British radio operator monitoring German military frequencies heard something she didn't recognize. Not the familiar Morse dit-dit-dah of Enigma traffic — something else. Her report called it "strange new music." It was encrypted teletype code, transmitted by a machine the British had never seen and couldn't name. They called it "Tunny."

The machine behind that signal was the Lorenz SZ40, built by a German company that had no idea its product would become the most important cipher system of the Second World War. Enigma, patented in 1920, was the famous cipher. Tunny was the one Hitler's High Command actually used for their most sensitive strategic communications — the ones that mattered.

Breaking Tunny required building something that didn't exist yet: a large-scale electronic computer. The machine that resulted, Colossus, was designed by a Post Office engineer named Tommy Flowers. Not a mathematician. Not a university professor. An engineer who'd previously built codebreaking equipment for Alan Turing, and who spent eleven months and his own department's budget constructing a room-sized, one-tonne machine filled with 2,400 vacuum tubes.

The establishment didn't believe it would work. Bletchley Park's leadership thought vacuum tubes were too unreliable for sustained operation. Flowers built it anyway, largely at the Post Office research station at Dollis Hill, and delivered it to Bletchley in December 1943. It worked. It worked so well that they ordered ten more.

Here's what Colossus actually did: it performed statistical analysis on intercepted German cipher traffic at electronic speed, finding patterns that human analysts could never spot in time. The Lorenz cipher used a complex system of rotating wheels — twelve of them, compared to Enigma's three or four — and the only way to break it was to try enormous numbers of combinations and look for statistical anomalies. Colossus didn't "compute" in the modern sense. It was a specialized, single-purpose machine. But it was programmable — you could reconfigure its behavior by changing switch settings and plugboard connections — and it was electronic, which meant it operated at speeds mechanical machines couldn't touch.

The intelligence Colossus produced was called "Tunny decrypts," and it was some of the highest-grade intelligence of the war. These weren't tactical field reports. These were strategic communications between Berlin and army group commanders across Europe. The decrypts informed decisions at the highest level — including the planning of D-Day. Bletchley Park's own assessment was that Tunny intelligence "changed the course of the war in Europe" and saved "an incalculable number of lives."

That's the heroic version. Here's the part that should make you angry.

At the end of the war, Winston Churchill ordered that all ten Colossus machines be destroyed. Not stored. Not archived. Destroyed — broken into pieces no larger than a man's hand, and the pieces burned. The blueprints were burned too. Tommy Flowers was told to never speak of it again, and he didn't. The women who operated Colossus — Dorothy Du Boisson, Elsie Booker, and many others whose names we don't know — were bound by the Official Secrets Act and said nothing for decades.

The destruction wasn't paranoia. It was policy. The British government reasoned that if the Soviet Union knew the extent of British codebreaking capability, they'd change their own cipher systems — many of which used similar Lorenz-derived technology. So Colossus was sacrificed to protect a secret that was already, in some cases, compromised. The knowledge that could have accelerated computing by years was incinerated to preserve an intelligence advantage of uncertain duration.

Tommy Flowers went back to the Post Office. He tried to get funding for a general-purpose electronic computer — the kind of machine Colossus had proven was possible. He was turned down. The government wouldn't fund it, and the Post Office wasn't in the computer business. In America, ENIAC was built in 1945 and became the machine everyone remembered as the "first computer." ENIAC was a remarkable achievement, but it was also a machine that Colossus had preceded by two years and that existed in part because British codebreaking successes had been quietly shared with American allies.

The history of computing was written without Colossus in it. For thirty years, the standard story was that electronic computing began with ENIAC in 1945. Books, courses, and documentaries all told this story. It was wrong. The real first machine was sitting in a classified file that nobody could access, built by a man who couldn't talk about it, operated by women whose contributions were erased by official secrecy.

The information started leaking in the 1970s. Brian Randell, a computer scientist, published a paper in 1976 that pieced together the Colossus story from declassified fragments. The full picture didn't emerge until the 1990s and 2000s, when more documents were released and some of the surviving operators finally spoke publicly. By then, the narrative had hardened. ENIAC was in every textbook. Colossus was a footnote that most computer scientists had never heard of.

This September, the IEEE will dedicate a Milestone plaque at Bletchley Park commemorating Colossus as the first large-scale programmable electronic digital computer. It's a meaningful gesture — the IEEE Milestone program recognizes achievements of historical significance in electrical engineering and computing. But it's also a gesture that's eighty years late.

The question worth asking is: what else don't we know? Not about Colossus — that story is mostly recovered now, thanks to the work of historians like B. Jack Copeland and the surviving operators who finally got to tell their stories. The question is about the pattern. Colossus was destroyed because the people in power valued a short-term intelligence advantage over long-term scientific progress. They made a rational decision by their own lights. And it cost the field of computing — by some estimates — five to ten years of progress.

We like to think of innovation as a smooth upward curve. It isn't. It's a curve with gaps — places where knowledge existed and was deliberately destroyed, suppressed, or classified beyond the point where it could do anyone outside a small circle any good. The gaps are invisible from the future. You can't look at a timeline and see what's missing, because what's missing was never written down. It was burned.

Colossus matters not just because it was first, but because it's a reminder that "first" is a political category, not just a chronological one. ENIAC was first because it was public. Colossus was first and then un-first because someone in authority decided that the future should wait. The lesson isn't that secrecy is always wrong — wartime secrecy is a genuine trade-off, and the Tunny decrypts were genuinely valuable. The lesson is that the costs of secrecy are invisible, and the people who pay them are not the people who decide to impose them.

Tommy Flowers died in 1998. He received an honorary doctorate late in life, but no knighthood, no major public recognition during his productive years. The machine he built was ahead of its time, and then it was ash, and then it was a secret, and now it's a plaque. Eighty-three years after it worked, and fifty-seven years after it was destroyed, the world is finally putting up a sign that says: this happened here.

It's not enough. But it's a start.
