*Every shepherd who adds one more sheep to the commons is making a reasonable personal choice. The pasture is still green, and the extra wool helps the family. The ruin comes later, when everyone has made the same reasonable choice and the grass is gone. We have a name for this pattern in land, fisheries, and air. We are only beginning to see how it applies to skill itself.*

---

## A commons no one owns

The phrase "tragedy of the cognitive commons" sounds like the kind of thing a worried professor invents to get quoted. But Nolan Lovett's recent paper of the same name lands on a real structural problem: cognitive work is becoming a shared resource that nobody is responsible for maintaining. The resource is professional expertise — the accumulated, renewed, judgment-laden knowledge that lets a field keep solving its hardest problems. The shepherds are firms, professionals, trainees, and tools, each making locally rational decisions that may, in aggregate, eat the grass.

Lovett's framework is straightforward and uncomfortable. There are two ways to be good at something. **Internalized mastery** is what you build by doing hard work over time: the surgeon who has felt enough tissue to know when something is wrong before the scan shows it; the engineer who can look at a circuit and smell a race condition; the lawyer who has lost enough cases to know which arguments sound brilliant and hollow. **Distributed mastery** is what you build by orchestrating tools: the same professional, now routing judgments through AI assistants, templates, checklists, and dashboards.

Both have their place. Distributed mastery is how modern work scales. No one wants their hospital to run on one surgeon's intuition, or their bridge to depend on one engineer's memory. But the two forms of mastery are not interchangeable. Internalized mastery is what lets you *supervise* distributed mastery without becoming a passenger. It is also, inconveniently, what gets depleted when too many people skip the hard years.

## The validation tether

The sharpest idea in Lovett's paper is the **validation tether**. Effective oversight of a tool depends on the expertise the tool might be replacing. You can only catch a model's bad answer in your domain if you still know enough of the domain to recognize the bad answer. The tether gets shorter every time a junior person skips the foundational work because a tool handles it. It gets shorter every time a senior person delegates the hard judgment to a screen. Eventually the tether is too short to hold anything.

This is not an abstract worry. In medicine, residents who train with heavy diagnostic assistance sometimes reach attending level without the same pattern-recognition depth as previous cohorts. In software, teams that rely on generated code can ship faster while losing the debugging instincts that come from having written the hard parts yourself. In law, document review tools can process millions of pages, but the associates who would have learned the case law by reviewing them now learn less. Each case is a productivity win. The aggregate is a slow leak in the profession's reservoir of judgment.

The cruel part is that the leak is invisible for years. A field does not collapse the first time a junior worker uses a copilot. It collapses — or fragments, or becomes brittle — when a critical mass of practitioners can operate tools but can no longer independently evaluate what the tools produce. By then the training pipelines have already adapted. The apprenticeships have thinned. The old guard has retired. Restoring the commons takes longer than depleting it.

## Why this is a policy problem, not just a training problem

Lovett's paper reframes the issue away from the usual "upskilling" talk. Upskilling is fine as far as it goes, but it treats expertise as an individual employee problem: if workers fall behind, send them to a course. The commons framing says expertise is a collective, intergenerational resource. A profession needs enough people at each stage of development to teach the next stage. When that chain breaks, no amount of individual training fixes it, because the people who would do the training are also gone or diluted.

This moves the conversation to governance. Organizations can design workflows that preserve apprenticeship even while using tools. Professional associations can set standards for what human practitioners must still be able to do without assistance. Regulators can require baseline competence testing in fields where public risk is high. Universities and training programs can resist the pressure to replace hard exercises with tool-assisted shortcuts. None of this is anti-technology. It is the boring, institutional work of maintaining a commons.

The alternative is what we usually do: optimize locally and hope the long-term problem belongs to someone else. The firm that cuts junior hiring because AI can handle the routine work does not pay the cost when, fifteen years later, there are no senior practitioners left to validate the next generation of tools. That cost lands on the profession, on patients and clients, on the public, and on the next cohort of trainees who thought they were entering a field, not a workflow.

## What internalized mastery actually is

There is a temptation to romanticize internalized mastery as nostalgia for the pre-computer age. That misses the point. The question is not whether to use tools but whether the tools are being used in a way that preserves the ability to operate without them.

Internalized mastery is not memorization. It is the ability to reason from first principles when the tool is wrong, slow, or unavailable. It is the map of the territory that lets you notice when the GPS has sent you into a lake. A pilot with internalized mastery does not refuse the autopilot; she knows why the autopilot's recommendation makes sense, and she can take over when it does not. A mathematician who uses a proof assistant still needs to know what a valid proof feels like. A coder who uses generated completions still needs to understand the system well enough to debug it at 2 AM when the model is down and the pager is ringing.

This is why the argument "the tool is better than most humans" is only half relevant. Even if the tool is better on average, you still need humans who can tell when it is not better in this specific case. That judgment is what the commons supplies. Judgment is not a plugin you install at the last mile. It is the residue of years of struggle with problems that were just barely within your reach.

## A stance, not a panic

None of this means we should slow down AI adoption out of fear. The productivity gains are real. The accessibility gains are real. A world where a nurse in a rural clinic can get a second opinion from a diagnostic tool, or where a solo lawyer can draft a contract that would once have required a team, is a better world in important ways. The question is whether we are building those gains on sand or stone.

The cognitive commons view says: treat expertise as infrastructure. Maintain it the way you maintain roads, power grids, or water systems. That means some inefficiency is acceptable. It means protecting the slow, expensive parts of training even when a tool could make them cheaper. It means valuing the people who carry the field's memory and judgment enough to keep them in the pipeline, not just the ones who operate the latest interface.

There is also a practical, self-interested reason to care. Tools change fast. The interface you optimize for today will be obsolete in five years. The underlying skill — reading closely, reasoning carefully, judging quality, noticing anomaly — transfers. A workforce that keeps those skills is more adaptable. One that outsources them is more fragile, no matter how impressive its current dashboards look.

## What I think we should actually do

First, stop pretending the problem will solve itself through market pressure. Markets optimize for the near term. The regeneration of expertise is a long-term public good, and public goods need institutions.

Second, every profession should be asking a hard version of the same question: what must a person be able to do, alone and under pressure, to be considered a member of this field in good standing? The answer will differ for radiologists, electricians, historians, and pilots. The exercise matters because it forces the field to name what it cannot afford to lose.

Third, organizations using cognitive tools should be required to account for how they are training the next cohort, not just how they are serving this quarter's output. That may mean quotas for human-supervised work, protected time for apprenticeship, or audit trails that show what decisions were made by humans and what was delegated.

Fourth, individuals should be honest about what they are skipping. If you use a tool to generate text, code, analysis, or diagnosis, ask yourself whether you could have produced a defensible version without it. Not to be a purist, but to keep the tether long. The goal is not to refuse help. The goal is to remain the kind of person who knows when help has gone wrong.

## The commons we commit to

The real tragedy is not that AI will make us stupid. The real tragedy is that we could make ourselves competent passengers in systems we no longer understand, and we could do it one reasonable decision at a time. Each shortcut is small. Each productivity gain is real. The depletion is cumulative, slow, and mostly invisible until the field needs a generation of experts who never fully arrived.

Preventing that outcome does not require halting progress. It requires recognizing that expertise is a commons, not a commodity. Some resources cannot be owned. They can only be stewarded. The question is whether we will steward the cognitive commons deliberately or inherit the tragedy of its neglect.
